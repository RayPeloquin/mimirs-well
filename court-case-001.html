Recipe Pucks × SKEPTIC-1

An unexpected pairing from Grok’s Alchemist’s Bench trial: can a reversible probe help a person identify a kitchen control’s true state?

Proposed experiment · Not built or validated

Open prefilled test brief Read source record

What the two designs bring

Recipe Pucks supplies a proposed tangible selector, where a person’s understanding of a setting could differ from the machine’s state. Its archived illustration does not yet settle the precise function of a puck; there is no demonstrated hardware or human testing.

SKEPTIC-1 supplies an idea from an ideal numerical mechanical model: a reversible off-center intervention can distinguish otherwise mirror-ambiguous faults. This is an analogy, not evidence that the mechanical technique already works in kitchen controls.

Proposed mechanism

A person deliberately triggers a short, reversible one-sided haptic or detent cue. The controller maps its verified current internal state to a distinguishable response, then returns the control to neutral. A mere off-center vibration that is identical for every state would reveal nothing.

The three experimental conditions

A · Baseline

Ordinary Recipe Pucks selector. No extra probe or state-confirmation action.

Measures baseline state-identification errors and time.

B · Asymmetric probe

Same selector with a user-triggered, reversible, state-dependent one-sided tactile cue.

Tests whether the asymmetric intervention helps.

C · Conventional confirmation

Same selector with a plain, accessible “Show current state” button providing the same verified state information.

Tests whether ordinary feedback explains the benefit.

Decisive question: Does B outperform C, not merely A? If B beats A but not C, the evidence supports extra state information; it does not establish a special advantage for asymmetry.

How to test it

  1. Specify the actual selector states and ensure B’s cue reliably distinguishes them; verify that C reports each state correctly.
  2. Pre-register minimum useful error reduction, acceptable time cost, task order and analysis. Explain intentional visual ambiguity in consent and debriefing.
  3. Counterbalance A, B and C across participants and compare both clear and intentionally ambiguous displays with equivalent practice and task difficulty.
  4. Record true-state identification error, time to correct state, total task completion time, accidental selections, cue usage and confusion.
  5. Compare B against both A and C with uncertainty estimates, noting mixed and inconclusive outcomes rather than labeling them successes.

What remains unknown

Neither a functioning Recipe Pucks selector nor a physical SKEPTIC-1 apparatus has been documented here. No human trial, haptic perception result, reliability claim, error reduction, or usability advantage has been established. A safe state sensor, actuator, accessible feedback and fail-neutral behavior would all need engineering and testing.

Grok’s verdict: Not ready as a product; potentially worth a small, inexpensive experiment in human–machine disambiguation.

Provenance and credit
