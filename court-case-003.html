Court case study 003 · 23 November 2026

CRUCIBLE × ECHO-Rx

Can a controller obey every rule now, yet violate a safety limit later because its previous action keeps having effects?

Proposed simulation · No hardware or clinical validation

Open the prefilled test brief Read technical research record

The unexpected connection

CRUCIBLE tests rule guarding under synthetic rover and quadrotor stress. Its rover campaign reports 8/40 legacy violations versus 0/40 guarded; the separate quadrotor campaign reports 31/40 versus 27/40, including a regression. None is a field safety guarantee.

ECHO-Rx demonstrates in a mathematical toy model why stopping new production need not halt the effect of material already released. This is not validated biology.

Grok transferred the lesson, not the mechanism: a legal present action can leave a residual that produces an illegal future state.

Proposed residual-aware controller

Track a residual state alongside ordinary position, velocity or temperature. Before allowing a new action, predict the residual tail and its uncertainty. Reject, derate or stop the action early enough that the predicted reachable states remain within the constraint envelope—even after a future stop command.

Important: Tracking must begin before a stop. Stopping the command does not cancel stored momentum, heat, delay or released material. Finite-horizon prediction also needs a defensible tail bound beyond the modeled window.

Three experimental controls

A · Present-state guard

CRUCIBLE-style immediate rule check, without future residual prediction.

Measures violations caused by lingering effects.

B · Explicit residual budget

Same guard plus a residual estimate, forecast safety envelope and explainable budget-triggered earlier restrictions.

Tests the modular residual safeguard.

C · Standard predictive safety

Conventional prediction-based safety controller with access to the same residual dynamics and uncertainty information.

Checks whether B offers value beyond prior control methods.

B versus A tests if looking ahead helps. B versus C tests if the separately auditable residual-budget approach adds an engineering advantage rather than rediscovering predictive control.

Proposed simulation

  1. Reproduce the rover and quadrotor baseline records separately, retaining exact source and test versions.
  2. Extend held-out scenarios with physical analogues such as continued momentum, actuator delay or stored thermal energy.
  3. Pre-register safety boundaries, model-mismatch cases, prediction horizon, sample count, severity measures and acceptable extra stops.
  4. Run A/B/C on matched disturbance traces: ordinary operation, stop command with a continuing tail, delayed actuation, partial observation and unexpected persistence.
  5. Measure post-stop violations, overshoot, violation duration, budget-triggered interventions, unnecessary early stops, task completion and computational cost.
  6. Evaluate robustness to noise and inaccurate residual models; do not call zero observed violations proof of perfect safety.

Prior art and evidence boundary

Predictive control, delay compensation and control barrier methods already address future-state constraint violations. See Control barrier functionals: Safety-critical control for time delay systems (DOI 10.1002/rnc.6751) and Constrained control of multi-input systems with distinct input delays (DOI 10.1002/rnc.7187).

The narrowly testable possibility is a lightweight residual ledger integrated into an auditable guarded controller. Whether that is useful or distinct from standard predictive safety control is not yet known.

Current verdict: Worth a controlled simulation study, not a physical build or safety claim.
