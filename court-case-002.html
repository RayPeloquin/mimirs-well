Ghostglass × Chihuahua Sphere

Can independent source-to-receiver energy accounting expose false weak-signal detections that survive statistical testing—and outperform ordinary instrument quality checks?

Proposed experiment · Not physically validated

Open the prefilled Bench test Read research record

Two disciplines against self-deception

Ghostglass provides a synthetic on/off statistical reference with 15 committed software tests passed, not a physically validated photonic detector.

Chihuahua Sphere / CS-1 supplies a calculation-only energy-accounting discipline: electrical input, emitted light, path losses and received energy belong in separate accounts rather than being counted as independent outputs.

Grok combined the two into a proposed calibration-integrity workflow. The idea is testable, not yet demonstrated or established as novel.

The measurement trap

A ledger copied from the detector under test is not independent evidence. The proposed system needs separately calibrated source output and defensible throughput/loss estimates, plus a detector-response model with uncertainties. A mismatch should flag a run for further review, not automatically declare it fraudulent or physically impossible. A broad energy bound may add no useful discrimination for very weak signals.

Three experimental conditions

A · Statistics alone

Pre-registered Ghostglass-style on/off analysis and necessary basic operation checks.

Baseline false-positive rate and true-positive sensitivity.

B · Independent energy ledger

Same statistics plus separately calibrated emitted optical energy, path losses and receiver-energy model, with uncertainty-aware discrepancy flags.

Tests incremental value of dual bookkeeping.

C · Standard detector QC

Same statistics plus conventional dark-count, gain, reference, drift and leakage checks, without the proposed dual-ledger closure rule.

Tests whether B adds more than established controls.

Key comparison: B against both A and C at matched true-positive sensitivity. Rejecting every trial would reduce false alarms but would not constitute a useful detector.

How to test it fairly

  1. Use a safe calibrated low-power optical source, receiver and separate optical reference; establish the source, attenuation, collection and uncertainty model before testing.
  2. Randomize known-null-target trials, deliberately injected artifacts such as leakage or gain drift, and known positive-signal trials. Source-on background is not itself proof of the intended target signal.
  3. Freeze detection thresholds, ledger discrepancy bounds, ordinary QC rules and analysis method before reviewing blinded outcome labels.
  4. Run the same underlying trial data through A, B and C, recording false alarms, true-positive detection, energy residual uncertainty, inconclusive results and added test burden.
  5. Compare false-alarm rates at comparable signal detection rates; investigate whether any B advantage persists over C and after uncertainty and real-world drift are included.

Limits and decision rule

Potentially useful: A pre-specified improvement in false-positive rate for B over both A and C, without unacceptable sensitivity loss or cost, would justify further work.

Not distinctive: B beats A but not C. That would support careful calibration and quality control, not a unique dual-ledger advantage.

Inconclusive: Results depend on circular measurements, unmodeled losses, uncalibrated detector counts or wide uncertainty. No physical test of this crossover has yet been documented.

Provenance
